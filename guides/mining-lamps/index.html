Collecting miners' lamps: types, makers and value

Updated 12 November 2026 · Free template and reference catalogue

Miners' lamps are one of the most characterful things you can collect: two hundred years of safety engineering in brass and steel, often marked with a maker and the very colliery they lit. They are also easy to mistake one for another, because a common 1950s pit lamp and a rare early safety lamp can look alike at a glance. This guide covers the main types, the makers to know, how to date a lamp, and what drives value, with a free spreadsheet to start cataloguing today.

The main lamp types

Almost every miners' lamp falls into one of these families. Knowing which you are holding is the first step to dating and valuing it.

TypeEraHow it worksCollector note
Open flame
candle, oil wick, tallow		1700s to mid 1800sA naked flame on a spike or in an open oil pan. The "sticking tommy" candle holder is the classic example.Early and simple. Valued as mining history more than for fine finish.
Davyfrom 1815Flame enclosed in a fine wire gauze that cools escaping gas below the ignition point.Named after Sir Humphry Davy. Genuine early examples are scarce and sought after.
Clannyfrom 1813Glass cylinder around the flame with gauze above it, so it is brighter than a Davy.Dr William Reid Clanny of Sunderland. The pattern most later lamps built on.
Stephenson (Geordie)from 1815Glass cylinder inside the gauze with a perforated metal cap. Developed independently of Davy.George Stephenson. A north east favourite, hence "Geordie".
Bonneted (Mueseler, Marsaut)1840s to 1900sA Clanny type under a metal bonnet, often with several gauzes and an inner chimney for use in draughts.The mainstream British colliery flame lamp into the 20th century.
Naphtha relighter (Wolf)from 1883Burns naphtha and carries an internal igniter, so it can be relit at the face without opening.Carl Wolf of Zwickau. Found marked Wolf or Friemann & Wolf.
Carbide (acetylene)c.1900 to 1950sWater drips onto calcium carbide to make acetylene, giving a bright white flame. Cap and hand versions.Common in metal mining and American coal. Justrite is the name you will see most.
Electric cap lampfrom the 1910sA battery pack on the belt feeds a lamp on the helmet, with a charging tag and serial plate.CEAG, Oldham and Edison dominate. Took over from flame and carbide from the 1930s.

Makers worth knowing

A legible maker mark is one of the best things a lamp can have. These are the makers that come up most, with the patterns and dates to expect.

MakerWhere and whenKnown for
Protector Lamp & LightingEccles, England · 1870s to todayThe most commonly found British colliery flame lamp. Patterns include the Type 6, GR6, SL and M&Q. Still trading, so later examples are plentiful.
E. Thomas & WilliamsAberdare, Wales · 1860s to todayThe "Cambrian" Welsh lamp. Still makes polished brass lamps, so many bright examples are modern and decorative rather than pit used.
Hailwood & AckroydMorley, Leeds · 1880s to mid 1900sImproved Clanny and combustion tube flame lamps.
Wolf / Friemann & WolfSheffield and Zwickau · 1880s onNaphtha relighter flame lamps and carbide lamps.
PattersonGateshead, England · late 1800s to 1900sNorth east flame safety lamps.
CEAGBarnsley, England · 1910s onElectric cap lamps and battery sets.
Oldham & SonDenton, England · 1920s onElectric cap lamps and rechargeable batteries.
KoehlerMarienville, USA · early to mid 1900sAmerican permissible flame safety lamps.

Watch for reproductions. Bright, heavily polished Welsh brass lamps are often modern decorative pieces, not antiques. Genuine pit used lamps usually show honest wear, a legible maker stamp, and a working lock. Originality beats shine.

What to record for each lamp

You do not need every field on day one, but these are the ones that matter when you want to search, value or insure the collection later. The template and the Hoardr Mining Lamps category both have a place for each.

  • Lamp type. Flame safety, carbide, electric cap lamp and so on, from the families above.
  • Maker and maker marks. Who made it, and where the mark sits: base, fuel vessel, lock or plaque.
  • Model or pattern. Type 6, GR6, Cambrian and the like.
  • Fuel. Oil, naphtha, carbide or electric. This pins the era as much as anything.
  • Materials. Brass, steel or alloy, which is a strong dating clue.
  • Lock type. Screw, magnetic or lead seal.
  • Serial or colliery tag. Numbers and pit tags can sometimes be traced to a mine or year.
  • Mine or provenance, if known, and how you know it.
  • Date or era, with the reasoning, plus what you paid and what it is worth.

How to date a lamp

No single clue dates a lamp on its own, but together they narrow it quickly.

  • Materials. Heavy brass points to Victorian, Edwardian or early 20th century. Pressed steel and light alloy suggest after the First World War or mid century. Bakelite or plastic means after the 1930s.
  • Lock and seal. Simple screw tops and hooks are earlier. Magnetic locks and lead seals are later and tamper resistant.
  • Tags and plates. British National Coal Board tags are typically mid 20th century. Inspection plaques and service stamps can sometimes be traced to a pit.
  • Maker dates. Look up when the company operated and which patterns it ran. A maker that closed in 1950 cannot have made a 1970 lamp.

What a lamp is worth

These are indicative bands only. The real measure of a lamp is what a comparable one has recently sold for, not what hopeful listings ask.

LampIndicative value
Common 20th century British brass and steel flame safety lamp (Protector and similar)about A$80 to A$250
Flame safety lamp in good order, c.1880 to 1920about A$270 to A$330
Justrite brass carbide lamp, c.1910 to 1930about A$110 to A$140
Genuine early named type (Davy, Clanny, Stephenson), rare maker or named mine provenancehundreds to thousands
Modern decorative Welsh brass lamp (reproduction)about A$30 to A$80

Value climbs with originality over restoration, an intact gauze and glass, a working lock and lighter, a legible maker mark and serial or colliery tag, a named mine, brass over pressed steel, and completeness down to the hook, wick adjuster or carbide burner. If the collection is worth real money, it is worth documenting properly for insurance, which is its own job.

Looking after them

  • Keep the patina. Collectors value honest age over a mirror polish, and aggressive polishing lowers value.
  • Keep them dry. Damp is the enemy of brass and steel, so store them out of humidity.
  • Do not convert them. Turning an antique lamp into mains lighting destroys both safety and value.
  • Mind the glass and gauze. These are the parts that break and go missing, and a complete lamp is worth much more.
